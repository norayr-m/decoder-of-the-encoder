What it is

A self-contained browser visualization that takes Bach’s Fugue No. 2 in C minor, BWV 847 from The Well-Tempered Clavier, Book I (1722), and lays it out as a navigable tree. Each voice of the fugue becomes a branch, each note becomes a leaf, and the temporal structure of the piece — subject entries, inversions, augmentations, stretto — becomes a topology you can walk through with the mouse.

The point is not the visual effect. The point is that you can navigate the structure of a 300-year-old piece of music as if it were a graph, and the navigation is faithful to what’s actually in the score. As the audio plays, the corresponding nodes in the tree light up; you can click into a voice’s subtree and follow its line independently; you can step back up via breadcrumb navigation.

Open rank-sphere.html in a browser. Type 847.

The whole demo is a single self-contained HTML file with no build step, no server, and no external dependencies beyond a modern browser. Audio is opt-in.

Why it matters — bio digital twins

A bio digital twin is a computational model of a specific biological system — a section of cortex, a hepatic lobule, a heart chamber — wired tightly enough that it can predict the system’s response to a stimulus. The honest question this project asks at the framework level is: when you have a complex object that was built by composition (one operator applied many times under disciplined transformations), what is the right way to decompose it back into its parts so that the decomposition is faithful and inspectable?

A fugue is the cleanest concrete example of that compose-then-decompose loop in the wild. The fugue subject is a single short musical idea. The fugue itself is the subject repeated across several voices, each transformed: transposed, inverted, augmented, delayed. To listen to a fugue is, for the trained ear, to do this decomposition in real time — pick out the subject, recognise the transformation, hear how the voices interact.

This visualization makes that decomposition mechanical and visual. The same encoder/decoder symmetry shape appears throughout the bio digital twin work: a complex tissue state was produced by composing many local cellular operations; the question is whether you can decompose the observed state back into the per-cell contributions that produced it. The fugue is a 300-year-old artistic instance of that same shape that you can listen to.

The framing for the trio (generator + cell simulator + scanner) is the trio white paper. This paper drills into the decoder-of-the-encoder visualization specifically as a standalone illustration.

How it works — fugue voices as branches

The fugue subject is the musical equivalent of a function f. Each voice i of the fugue applies a projection Πi to the subject — a transposition, an inversion, an augmentation in duration, a delay in entry — producing the voice’s local rendition of the subject. The complete fugue is then the aggregate of all the voices: each voice playing its transformed copy, with the contrapuntal rules controlling how the voices relate to each other in time.

The visualization mirrors that structure as a tree:

  • The root of the tree is the fugue itself — the whole piece.
  • The first level of branches is the set of voices.
  • Inside each voice, the next level is the set of subject entries for that voice — every place where the voice introduces a new transformed copy of the subject.
  • Inside each subject entry, the leaves are the individual notes that make up that entry.

When the audio plays, the engine walks down the tree in time-synchronised fashion: the nodes whose notes are firing now light up. When you click a node, you descend into that subtree — you can isolate a single voice and follow its line, or you can isolate a single subject entry and watch how it interacts with the others. Breadcrumb navigation walks back up.

The visual extras — the Penrose-chainmail curtain that parts on entry, the fiber-optic light strands that trace voice entries through the tree, the rank-based rendering that mutates from clean scaffolding into Escher-style impossibility at depth — are aesthetic; the structural mapping (voice → branch, entry → subtree, note → leaf) is the load-bearing claim.

Reading the visualization

When you open the file and type 847:

  • The piece begins playing (audio is opt-in; mute is available).
  • The complete tree is laid out across the canvas. Voices are visually distinct.
  • As notes fire in the audio timeline, the corresponding leaves light up.
  • Subject entries are highlighted across their full subtree as they begin.
  • Click any node to descend into its subtree; the rest of the tree fades.
  • Breadcrumb navigation (top of the screen) walks you back up.

What to look for:

  • Subject persistence. The subject is the same musical idea every time it appears, but you can see (and hear) it transformed each time. Watching the tree light up across multiple voices simultaneously is watching the same idea persist across different transformations.
  • Stretto. When two voices enter with the subject very close together in time, the tree shows the two subtrees overlapping in their lighting pattern. This is visually obvious in a way it is not in a printed score.
  • Inversion. Inverted entries flip the contour of the subject. The tree’s leaves for an inverted entry move in the opposite direction from the original.
  • Counterpoint. While one voice is in the middle of the subject, the other voices are running their own material that has to satisfy harmonic constraints against the subject. The tree shows all voices simultaneously, so you can see the constraints being satisfied in real time.

The companion — drt-pipeline

The companion repository drt-pipeline runs a forward-and-backward pass on the same BWV 847 example: forward direction is composition (subject → polyphonic fugue), backward direction is decomposition (polyphonic fugue → subject + per-voice transformations). The two demos share the same musical example and the same conceptual frame; this visualization (decoder-of-the-encoder) renders the navigable structure, and the pipeline visualization renders the symmetric forward/backward flow. They are deliberately released as a pair.

If the framework’s question is “can the aggregate be decomposed back into its parts?”, the decoder-of-the-encoder shows you the answer for the fugue specifically: yes, the structure is fully recoverable, and you can navigate it by hand. The pipeline shows you the symmetry of that recovery as a visual flow.

What is not in the paper

The honest list of what this paper deliberately does not claim:

  • An original musicology contribution. The mapping from fugue voices to tree branches is faithful to the score, not a novel interpretation of the music. Music theorists have analyzed BWV 847 for three centuries. This visualization makes the analysis interactive; it does not extend it.
  • A norm-growth claim. Earlier prose around this family of demos asserted that the aggregate of many partial views had bigger norm than the original signal — that ∥R(f)∥ > ∥f∥. That inequality has been retracted in the v0.1 draft. The current claim is conditional and weaker: under four hypotheses and a bounded computational budget, the aggregate exposes structural features that are not accessible from the original signal alone within the same budget. The fugue is an artistic example of that compose-decompose pattern; it is not a proof of the conditional claim.
  • A general theorem about decoders. The framework discusses return operators Ci that complete a partial projection back to a global estimate. The fugue’s voices are a clean instance of that pattern with the harmonic rules of counterpoint as the per-voice “completion” rule. That does not generalize to a theorem about arbitrary decoders; the framework’s general statements are conditional on hypotheses spelled out in the v0.1 draft.
